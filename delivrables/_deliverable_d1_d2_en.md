This note documents the methodology applied in Deliverables 1 and 2 of the assignment “Comparative analysis of effective, high-impact reuse/recovery practices” (WP1, Innovation Opportunities). ## 2. Framing decisions
## 2.1. Exclusion of Argentina, Chile and Colombia from the international universe
The three project countries were deliberately excluded from the universe of cases. ## 7. The push/pull dimension
A cross-cutting dimension was incorporated into the framework, classifying each circuit's instruments according to the force that moves the material:

- Push (supply side): instruments that push material into the circuit — mandatory separation,
consumer deposits, pay-as-you-throw, collection mandates, EPR capture targets.
- Pull (demand side): instruments that draw material from the destination — mandatory recycled
content, captive or premium demand, recovery certificates, guaranteed material prices. ## 8. Sources and verification protocol
## 8.1. Data hierarchy
Every figure in the record sheets is classified into one of three categories, explicitly flagged in the text:

- Consolidated data: from a primary or official source (public body, regulator, statistical report,
peer-reviewed publication), with an identifiable date.
- Estimate: figures from secondary literature or ranges with dispersion across sources; the range
and its endpoints are reported with attribution (e.g. the magnitude of catadores in Brazil, from 281 thousand per PNAD to 800 thousand–1 million per IPEA/MNCR).
- Company/organisation data: figures provided by the analysed actor itself; flagged “company
data: to be cross-checked” and not cited in formal deliverables without independent verification.

## 8.2. Verification statuses
Each case carries one of two statuses: “Verified” (cross-checked against online sources during July 2026, with consultation date) or “To be verified before delivery” (sheet built on consolidated literature, with specific figures flagged). The operating rule: no figure flagged “to be verified” is cited in a formal deliverable without prior cross-checking. At the close of this version, 21 of the 29 cases are verified — all firm selection candidates and the five incorporated valorisation cases — and the remaining 8 (all reserve cases) will be verified only if they survive the selection, applying the effort- where-there-is-return criterion.

Verification is not a formality: in this very research it changed conclusions. The adjustment of Carbios's timeline (announced in December 2025: commissioning postponed from 2026 to the first half of 2028) and the contradictory evaluations of the Ecuadorian redeemable tax are findings that only emerged from cross-checking recent sources.

## 10. Methodological limitations
The known limitations of the approach are recorded for consideration by the approval bodies:

- Documentation bias: the cases most cited in the international (and English-language) literature
are over-represented in the available evidence; valuable, less documented experiences may have been left out of the universe.
- Self-interested data: in the private cases (families D and E in particular) much of the metrics
comes from the companies themselves; the section 8 protocol mitigates but does not eliminate this risk.
- Cross-context comparability: performance rates are not directly comparable across countries
with different statistical definitions (what counts as “recycled” varies); the matrix therefore privileges the analysis of mechanisms over the ranking of figures.
- Ordinal coverage grading: the four-level scale is a documented expert judgement, not a
measurement; its reliability rests on traceability to each sheet's sources and on its review by the consortium.
- Non-exhaustive universe: 29 cases are a reasoned sample of the typological space, not a
census; the universe remains open to additions should the local analysis or the Search Conferences reveal unforeseen gaps.

### Family A — Deposit-refund systems (DRS)
Deposit-refund systems attack the chain through the direct economic incentive to the consumer: the container is worth money, and so it comes back. They are the instrument with the highest documented return rates, and the four selected cases cover the spectrum without redundancy: the German technical benchmark (which also documents the Norwegian performance-modulated eco- fee), the fast Lithuanian implementation, the Australian community model, and the Ecuadorian fiscal experiment — Latin America's only one, with its design lessons.

### A1 · Pfand / DPG (Germany)
Start/milestone: 2003 · Status: Verified 07/2026

The German deposit-refund system is the global benchmark for the instrument. Every single-use beverage container carries a €0.25 deposit that the consumer recovers by returning it to reverse vending machines (RVMs) installed in retail stores. The system has operated since 2003 on a 1991 regulatory framework and is run by a private industry-retail consortium (DPG) under public oversight.

The economic incentive solves the hardest stages of the chain in one stroke: the generator keeps and returns the container because it is worth money, the point of sale becomes the point of capture, and material reaches the counting plants with virtually no contamination, enabling high-quality bottle-to- bottle recycling. Return rates hold between 96% and 99%.

Lesson for the project. The case shows the instrument's ceiling and also its blind spot: the design excludes the informal actor (although a subsistence circuit collecting abandoned containers exists), and the unreturned material — barely 4% — remains as revenue retained by industry. For AR/CL/CO it is the technical benchmark against which to measure any DRS. A design variant worth noting (Norway/Infinitum): a producer eco-fee modulated by the system's collective return rate, aligning private interest with collective performance.

Key data: Return rate 96–99% (Federal Environment Agency, April 2026 report) · 'Pfandschlupf' (unreturned containers) ~4% estimated in 2015 = ~€180 M retained by industry
Sources (original names): Euronews (21/06/2026): 20 años del sistema de depósito alemán — tasas 96–99% · Asociación Retorna: experiencias internacionales de SDDR · eldiario.es: cómo funciona el Pfand; reforma 2022 · DPG Deutsche Pfandsystem GmbH (operador del sistema)

### A4 · Deposit system (USAD) (Lithuania)
Start/milestone: 2016 · Status: Verified 07/2026

Lithuania built its complete deposit system in record time: legislation in 2014, operator designated in 2015, launch in February 2016. The single not-for-profit operator (USAD, constituted by brewers, retail and water bottlers) manages a uniform €0.10 deposit on PET, glass and metal.

PET returns went from 34% (pre-system) to 74.3% in the first year, 91.9% in the second and 93% in 2018, holding above 90% since. A key deployment element: the technology provider (TOMRA) financed the return machines and recovers the investment through a per-container fee, removing the upfront capital barrier for retailers.

Lesson for the project. The empirical demonstration that a mid-sized country can go from zero to a world-class DRS in 24 months, with an infrastructure financing model directly replicable in capital- constrained contexts such as Argentina's.

Key data: Emblematic fast implementation: PET return from 34% (pre-system) to 74.3% at the close of the first year (2016), 91.9% in the second (2017) and 93% in 2018; sustained >90% since; ~1,000 RVM points + ~1,700 manual points; >600 M containers/year collected; single counting centre in Vilnius; 55,000 t CO₂/year avoided; EU 2025 targets met as early as 2017; >90% public approval

### A5 · Container Deposit Scheme (CDS) (Australia (South Australia))
Start/milestone: 1977 · Status: To be verified

South Australia has operated since 1977 the world's oldest continuously running deposit scheme (Beverage Container Act of 1975), with a 10-cent deposit per container. It was the model later replicated by the Northern Territory and the rest of the Australian states.

Unlike the European in-store machine model, the Australian system rests on a network of physical depots run by licensed private operators, which accept deliveries from any actor: individuals, informal collectors and community organisations (clubs, scouts) that gather containers to fund themselves.

Lesson for the project. The depot model is the DRS variant most compatible with existing social collection actors: where the RVM automates and excludes, the depot admits and integrates. For contexts with waste pickers it is the reference architecture.

Key data: Almost 50 years of continuous operation; historical return rates around 75–80% (current figure to be verified with Green Industries SA); the founding reference for all Australian container deposit schemes

### A6 · Redeemable Plastic Bottle Tax (IRBP) (Ecuador)
Start/milestone: 2012 · Status: Verified 07/2026

Ecuador is Latin America's only mass redeemable levy: since 2012, a USD 0.02 tax per non-returnable PET bottle, refunded to whoever delivers the container, administered by the tax authority (SRI). Declared unconstitutional in 2022 on formal grounds, it was reinstated by decree-law in 2023.

The instrument did not change generator habits — households generally do not separate — but it created a dense informal collection network and buy-back centres mobilised by the value of PET, and energised a transformation industry that previously did not exist in the country. RENAREC reports concrete income improvements for waste pickers.

Lesson for the project. The universe's most instructive case on incentive design: evaluations are contradictory (reported collection >100% of what is bottled, due to cross-border fraud; real recycling

### Family B — Mature EPR schemes
Extended producer responsibility schemes with decades of operation provide the most solid evidence on what EPR funds and what it does not. The five cases gather the two European producer- responsibility organisations with the greatest differential value (France and Italy) and the three Asian models that took separation discipline and institutional integration to their fullest expression (Japan, South Korea, Taiwan). From here come the universe's most transferable instruments: eco-modulation, per-tonne quality-based municipal payments and the stabilising fund with collector integration.

### B1 · Citeo (France)
Start/milestone: 1992 · Status: Verified 07/2026

Citeo is the French producer responsibility organisation for household packaging EPR: the private not- for-profit entity under state licence that, since 1992 (as Eco-Emballages), collects contributions from companies and uses them to fund selective collection in virtually every French municipality.

Its distinctive instrument is eco-modulation: each company's contribution rises or falls according to the recyclability of its packaging, its recycled content and, since 2025, its reduction efforts. The EPR fee thus becomes an upstream eco-design tool. Citeo also funds recycling R&D at 50% (enzymatic, pyrolysis, supercritical fluids) and created new industrial recycling clusters through tenders.

Lesson for the project. The case provides the universe's single most important expectation-setting figure: after 30 years of consolidated EPR, France recycles 73% of its packaging but only 27% of plastics (2023). EPR funds waste management; the technical recyclability of plastic additionally requires technology policy.

Key data: Packaging recycling rate (all materials) estimated at 73% at the close of 2025; 71.5 kg of packaging and paper separated per inhabitant; BUT plastics lag badly: 27% in 2023 (~30% currently, per Citeo), against 86% for glass and steel; new industrial clusters (PS yoghurt pots, PET trays, films) project +108,000 t recycled; October 2025 milestone: the world's first closed-loop yoghurt-pot recycling plant (with Indaver, Antwerp); €91 M in municipal support in 2024

### B4 · CONAI / COREPLA (Italy)
Start/milestone: 1997 · Status: Verified 07/2026

The Italian system is structured around the mandatory consortium CONAI (1997) and its plastics branch COREPLA, funded by the CONAI Environmental Contribution (CAC) paid by producers, differentiated into groups according to packaging recyclability.

Its distinctive mechanism is the ANCI-CONAI Agreement: the consortium pays each municipality corrispettivi (per-tonne fees) calibrated by the quality of the material delivered, with audits of the improper fraction. In 2019 it transferred €648 M to municipalities; over 92% of Italian municipalities hold agreements. In May 2026 the new 2026–2029 Framework Agreement entered into force, now also integrating the competing autonomous systems.

Lesson for the project. The case that most directly answers the central problem of the region's municipalities: how to finance separate collection. Per-tonne quality-based payment turns separation into an auditable municipal revenue rather than a cost.

### B5 · Containers and Packaging Recycling Law (Japan)
Start/milestone: 1995 · Status: To be verified

Japan's Containers and Packaging Recycling Law (1995, fully in force 2000) establishes an explicit legal division of burdens: the consumer separates, the municipality collects and conditions, and producers fund the recycling of the material they place on the market, through the designated association JCPRA.

The result is the world's highest separation-at-source discipline: households wash, remove labels and caps, and separate up to more than ten fractions depending on the municipality. Material arrives at plants practically pre-sorted — stage 2 does the work of stage 4 — and PET collection rates are among the highest on the planet.

Lesson for the project. The case demonstrates that the separation stage can absorb costs across the rest of the chain if the institutional architecture assigns responsibilities clearly. Its direct transferability is limited (it rests on social compliance norms), but the principle of a legal division of burdens is universal.

Key data: Exceptional separation-at-source discipline (up to 10+ fractions in some municipalities); PET collection rates among the world's highest (~90%+, JCPRA figure to be verified)
Sources (original names): JCPRA — Japan Containers and Packaging Recycling Association (cifras) · Ministerio de Ambiente de Japón: Containers and Packaging Recycling Law

### B6 · EPR + Volume-Based Waste Fee (South Korea)
Start/milestone: 1995 · Status: To be verified

South Korea combines two instruments in a single policy package: the Volume-Based Waste Fee (1995), under which each household pays per bag of mixed waste while recyclables are collected free, and a broad EPR scheme (2003) obliging producers through recycling cooperatives.

The VBWF is the world reference case for household-level economic incentive: paying for what is not separated proved the most powerful documented stimulus on household habits, with a drastic fall in mixed waste and municipal recycling rates above 60%. Enforcement (mandatory official bags, fines) sustains the system.

<!-- Page 27 -->
Lesson for the project. The VBWF is the deposit's mirror image: instead of rewarding returns, it makes non-separation expensive. It acts simultaneously on the generator (VBWF) and the producer (EPR) — the combination, more than either instrument alone, is what is transferable.

Key data: The world reference for household-level economic incentive: drastic reduction of mixed waste and municipal recycling above ~60% (KECO/OECD figure to be verified)
Sources (original names): OCDE: EPR y Volume-Based Waste Fee en Corea del Sur · KECO — Korea Environment Corporation · Ministerio de Ambiente de Corea del Sur

### B7 · 4-in-1 Recycling Program (Taiwan)
Start/milestone: 1997 · Status: Verified 07/2026

Taiwan's 4-in-1 programme (1997) turned the 'garbage island' into a world reference by legally integrating four actors: communities, collectors, municipalities and a state Recycling Fund financed by mandatory producer and importer fees.

The subsidy formula is explicit: the producer pays the difference between the cost of collecting and recycling each product and the revenue from selling the recovered material, externalities included — and only for marginally profitable items. The individual collector is integrated in three layers: indirectly (selling to licensed firms whose prices the Fund sustains), through targeted welfare (the 2019 Recycling Care Program: monthly subsidies of up to NT$5,000 for registered self-employed recyclers, with top-ups for the lowest-value materials — 20,936 beneficiaries in 2020) and municipally (assignment of recyclers to communities, e.g. Taichung). System results: 56.4% household recycling.

Lesson for the project. A comparative-analysis opportunity for AR/CL/CO: the Fund stabilises prices against international volatility and institutionalises academia–industry linkage; the Care Program contributes the exemplary mechanism — paying more for the material the market does not want. With precision: waste-picker integration is late (2019), limited in scale and welfare-style in logic; the most transferable element is the financial instrument, while the inclusion model is analysed in family C.

Key data: Celebrated transformation from 'garbage island' to world reference: 56.4% household recycling rate (25 years of the programme, Ministry of Environment); waste from 1.14 kg/person/day (1997) to ~0.4 (2011–2015); OECD (2015): among the world's highest (55%); regulation expanded to 14 categories / 38 items / 73 types, with 626 registered recycling firms (222 subsidised) and 88 disposal firms (78 subsidised) (MoE 2024); the Fund bought 1,300+ collection vehicles and funds university R&D; Recycling Care Program (2019): monthly subsidy NT$3,500→5,000 for registered self-employed recyclers, 20,936 beneficiaries in 2020 (6.1x vs. 2019), 9,215 t collected
Sources (original names): Ministerio de Ambiente de Taiwán (2021–2022): 25 años del 4-in-1 — 56,4% · MoE Taiwán (press release 2020): Recycling Management — Recycling Care Program, NT$3.500→5.000/mes · MoE Taiwán (press release 2021): 20.936 beneficiarios del Care Program en 2020, 9.215 t · MoE Taiwán (2024): Resource Recycling — 14 categorías/38 ítems/73 tipos; 626 empresas registradas · US EPA: Recycling Regulations in Taiwan and the 4-in-1 Recycling Program · AmCham Taiwan — Taiwan Topics (2019): From Garbage Island to Recycling Model (fondo: top-up de ítems marginales) · ScienceDirect (2024): modelo de asignación de waste pickers a comunidades — política de Taichung · Rapid Transition Alliance: fórmula del subsidio y génesis ciudadana (Homemakers United)

### Family C — Inclusive recycling and waste pickers
This family gathers the experiences where the waste picker is the protagonist: from full cooperative ownership (SWaCH) and nationwide legal recognition (Brazil) to pure informal efficiency (the Zabbaleen), through Uruguay's EPR with clasificadores, Brazilian civic-digital innovation and Mexico's voluntary EPR without a law. ### C1 · SWaCH (Solid Waste Collection & Handling) (India (Pune))
Start/milestone: 2005/2008 · Status: Verified 07/2026

SWaCH is the world's largest waste-picker cooperative: ~3,900 members (70% women, mostly from historically excluded communities) providing door-to-door collection for Pune, India, under an agreement (MoU) with the municipality — a 'public-private partnership' built from the KKPKP union in the 1990s.

The workers charge households a fee directly, sort manually with maximum value recovery and, since 2014, sell material directly to industry without intermediaries. They serve ~900,000 households (4 million people). The municipality saves ~USD 12.5 million per year — 46% of its solid-waste capital budget.

Lesson for the project. The case inverts the fiscal prejudice: cooperative formalisation did not make the service more expensive — it made it cheaper. The institutional pathway (union → cooperative → municipal agreement) is the world's most cited organisational model for the sector.

Key data: Serves ~900,000 households / 4 M people; estimated municipal saving of ~INR 900 M (USD 12.5 M) per year = 46% of the MSW capital budget; 2014 milestone: first direct sale of material to industry; 600,000+ citizen signatures supporting contract renewal

### C2 · PNRS + catadores (MNCR / Pró-Catador) (Brazil)
Start/milestone: 2010 · Status: Verified 07/2026

Brazil's National Solid Waste Policy (Law 12,305/2010) is the world's largest-scale legal recognition of waste pickers: it instructs municipalities to prioritise catador cooperatives and created federal support programmes (Pró-Catador, Cataforte). The sector's magnitude is disputed: from 281 thousand (PNAD 2019) to 800 thousand–1 million (IPEA/MNCR).

Catadores are estimated to account for ~90% of what is actually recycled in Brazil, and independent catadores' capture exceeds official selective collection by 1.6 times. Yet fifteen years after the law, only 1.7% of independents sell to cooperatives, 70% earn less than R$1,100 a month and two thirds receive no state benefit.

Lesson for the project. Brazil is a central experience for the Packaging Law debate: writing inclusion into the law is a necessary but not sufficient condition. Without effective municipal contracting, enforcement and a market, the legal mandate coexists with mass precarity.

### C3 · Plan VALE / Ley 19.829 (Uruguay)

### C4 · Zabbaleen (Egypt (El Cairo))

### D3 · Wecyclers (Nigeria (Lagos))
Start/milestone: 2012 · Status: To be verified

Wecyclers (Lagos, 2012) is a Nigerian social enterprise running incentivised door-to-door collection in low-income neighbourhoods, under agreement with the Lagos waste authority, awarded the King Baudouin African Development Prize (2019).

Its innovation is logistical: fleets of cargo bikes ('wecycles') that reach where trucks cannot, at minimal operating cost, and an SMS points programme rewarding households for delivered material — gamification that induced effective household separation in a context where no formal instrument had achieved it.

Lesson for the project. Ultra-low-cost last-mile logistics plus simple incentives: a combination transferable to popular neighbourhoods of mid-sized cities, complementary to (not a substitute for) formal collection and the waste-picker circuit.

Key data: Cargo-bike collection network with SMS incentives in Lagos's low-income neighbourhoods; King Baudouin African Development Prize 2019; operating metrics to be verified

### D4 · Banyan Nation (India (Hyderabad))
Start/milestone: 2013/2014 · Status: Verified 07/2026

Banyan Nation (Hyderabad, 2013/14, incubated at Columbia University) is India's reference technified recycler: it digitally integrates the informal kabadiwala (aggregator) chain — 10,000 mapped across 20 states — through its Mappr and Tradr platforms, registering every transaction with traceability to each kilo's origin.

### D5 · Recykal (India (Hyderabad, operación en 30+ estados))
Start/milestone: 2015 · Status: Verified 07/2026

Recykal (Hyderabad, 2015) is Asia's largest circular-economy marketplace: a B2B digital platform connecting 5,000+ aggregators (who buy from informal collectors) with 675+ certified recyclers and 620+ brands obliged by Indian EPR to demonstrate verifiable recovery.

The business is the traceability the law demands: transactions documented online (weighings, delivery notes), four-step compliance verification, sorting AI (CircularNet, with Google) above 90% polymer accuracy, and a digital deposit pilot (dDRS) with uniquely coded household bags. Lesson for the project. The key qualitative finding: India's formal recyclers state their bottleneck is not processing but sourcing reliable material — the exact coordination failure that intermediation resolves (badly, and asymmetrically) in Argentina. And the policy lesson: a well-enforced EPR creates a market for technological innovation.

Key data: Positions itself as Asia's largest circular-economy marketplace: ~50,000 t/month diverted from landfill (company data via Google Sustainability) · since 2023, ~12,000 M plastic bottles and 100,000+ t of metals transacted · 620–650+ brand clients

### Family E — Valorisation and new value chains
The family was deliberately reinforced from four to nine cases, as the critical stage for the regional context. It covers the full range of possible valorisation: open-source micro-scale (Precious Plastic) and micro-scale with a proven market (Gjenge Makers), industrial bottle-to-bottle (PetStar), Latin American construction taking any plastic (CRDC/RESIN8), clean-point pedagogy (TriCiclos), and the chemical frontier in its three states — operating at full scale (Eastman), starting up for flexibles and mixed waste (Mura, UBQ) and delayed with state support (Carbios). Together they expose each route's real requirements of capital, technology and demand.

### E1 · Precious Plastic (Red global (origen Netherlands))
Start/milestone: 2013 · Status: Verified 07/2026

Precious Plastic (2013, Netherlands) is a global open-source micro-recycling network: machine blueprints (shredder, extruder, injector, sheet press), tutorials and a marketplace published free of charge, which any actor can use to set up a local plastic valorisation workshop.

The community map registers 2,014 organisations worldwide. The self-reported sample of 184 (barely 9% of the total) recycled 1,400+ tonnes in 2023, generated USD 3.7 million in aggregate income and built and sold 1,175 machines — machine-making is itself a distributed industry. The network types its spaces: workshops, collection points, community points and machine shops.

Lesson for the project. Technology transfer in its purest, open form and the case most directly appropriable by the consortium's universities: a university FabLab can set up as a workshop, incubate ventures with cooperatives and even become a regional machine shop. Valorisation where industrial scale does not add up.

Key data: 2,014 organisations registered on the community map (2024); self-reported sample of 184 organisations (~9% of total): 1,400+ t recycled in 2023, USD 3.7 M aggregate income (average USD 25,000/year per project), 1,175 machines built and sold in 2023, participants from 56 countries; open-source v4 kit; the Bazar marketplace processed €200,000 as early as 2020

### E2 · PetStar (Mexico)

### E3 · Carbios (reciclado enzimático de PET) (France)
Start/milestone: 2024 (inicio de obra) · Status: Verified 07/2026

Carbios (France) is the biotech company that took enzymatic PET depolymerisation from the laboratory (CNRS/University of Toulouse enzymology) to the first industrial plant of its kind, in Longlaville, with €54 M in state subsidies (France 2030) and a JV with Indorama Ventures.

The technology recycles what mechanical recycling cannot — multilayer trays, opaque and coloured PET, polyester textiles — and returns monomers (terephthalic acid, TPA, and ethylene glycol, EG) for virgin-equivalent food-grade PET, infinitely recirculable. Offtake contracts (L'Oréal, L'Occitane, beverage industry) were signed before the plant operated; the technology is already licensed to Asia (Wankai).

Lesson for the project. High technical academia–industry linkage — with its warning included: in December 2025 commissioning was postponed from 2026 to the first half of 2028, pending financing. Frontier technologies carry a financial-temporal risk that public planning must budget for.

Key data: 50,000 t/year plant (~€230 M) · offtake contracts with L'Oréal, L'Occitane and beverage companies · ~50% of capacity pre-marketed · licence to Asia (Wankai) · timeline adjusted (Dec. 2025): commissioning 2026 → H1 2028, pending financing

### E5 · Eastman (molecular recycling / methanolysis) (USA (Kingsport, Tennessee))
Start/milestone: 2024 (producción inicial: marzo) · Status: Verified 07/2026

Eastman operates in Kingsport, Tennessee, the world's largest molecular recycling plant: 110,000 t/year methanolysis that depolymerises hard-to-recycle polyester — mechanical recyclers' rejects, carpets, coloured plastic — into the DMT monomer, the basis of virgin-quality specialty plastics. Initial production in March 2024, with an investment of ~USD 250 million.

In 2025 the plant produced 2.5 times more recycled content than in 2024, with its best uptime records. But the case carries two verified warnings: the Department of Energy withdrew the USD 375 million grant for the second plant (Texas) in 2025, and some durable-goods customers reverted to virgin resin on price — voluntary pull is fragile without regulation to sustain it.

Lesson for the project. The real counterpoint to Carbios: it proves advanced chemical recycling can operate at industrial scale today — and at the same time that its economics rest on two unstable supports: state backing (withdrawable) and brands' voluntary demand (reversible on price). The complete frontier case: proven capacity, fragile footing.

Key data: 110,000 t/year capacity, operating since March 2024; in 2025 produced 2.5x the recycled content of 2024, with best uptime; investment ~USD 250 M; demand counterpoint: in 2025 some durables customers REVERTED to virgin resin on price (Plastics News/Resource Recycling) — voluntary pull is fragile without regulation

### E6 · Mura Technology (Hydro-PRT, hydrothermal) (United Kingdom (Wilton,

Mura Technology built in Wilton (Teesside, UK) the world's first commercial hydrothermal recycling plant: its Hydro-PRT process uses supercritical water to convert, in ~30 minutes, contaminated flexible and multilayer plastics — the orphan material par excellence — into circular hydrocarbons for new production. The feedstock is exactly what no one else processes: films, multilayer packaging and mixed rigids with organic residues, which today go to incineration or landfill. The modular design (up to 4 lines, 80,000 t) and documented interest from petrochemical companies point to a global licensing model.

### E7 · UBQ Materials (Israel / Netherlands (Bergen op Zoom))
Start/milestone: 2012 (fundación) / 2024 (planta NL) · Status: Verified 07/2026

UBQ Materials (Israel) converts unsorted mixed municipal solid waste — organics, plastics, cardboard, even nappies — into a thermoplastic composite (UBQ™) usable as an additive or masterbatch in conventional manufacturing. Its full-scale plant in Bergen op Zoom (Netherlands, 2024) processes 104,600 t/year of waste into up to 80,000 t of material.

The process requires neither separation nor sorting: it takes the pre-treated stream (SRM/RDF) that would otherwise go to energy recovery or landfill. The material is sold as a price-stable alternative to resin volatility, with applications in logistics, automotive and even municipal waste carts in the US.

Lesson for the project. A case to discuss rather than to imitate: if a technological route makes separation and sorting unnecessary, what happens to chain stages 2–4 and to those who make a living from them? The uncomfortable and necessary question the Search Conferences should at least pose when thinking about the system's long term.

Key data: Bergen op Zoom: converts 104,600 t/year of MSW into up to 80,000 t of UBQ™; Israeli plant converted to R&D; US expansion under evaluation; 2025 portfolio reorganisation (discontinued Q Series and Impact lines, launched Masterbatch): a business model still finding its form

### E9 · CRDC Global / RESIN8 (Costa Rica (San José) + USA, South Africa and pilots in
7 countries) Start/milestone: 2018 (empresa) / 2022 (planta CR) · Status: Verified 07/2026

CRDC Global was born in Costa Rica as a beach-cleanup NGO (2010) and became a materials company: its patented process converts any plastic (resins 1 to 7), dirty and mixed, into RESIN8, an aggregate for concrete and asphalt meeting ASTM standards. The flagship San José plant (2022, up to 90 t/day) was co-developed with Grupo Pedregal, the country's leading construction group, with support from the Alliance to End Plastic Waste.

The model is locally replicable at scale: plants serving a bounded radius, fed by low-friction community collection (household 'all plastics' bags, direct drop-off). A second plant in Pennsylvania, a third in Cape Town, pilots in seven countries. Concrete with RESIN8 is up to 15% lighter and adds ~0.11% to a project's cost; Habitat for Humanity already uses it in Latin American social housing.

Lesson for the project. Probably the universe's most transferable valorisation case for AR/CL/CO: it accepts the material nobody wants (a demand floor for plastic without a market), the local industrial partner is the sales channel, and construction — a universal sector — can absorb mass volumes at a marginal cost premium. A direct candidate for the Deliverable 3 complementarity analysis.

Key data: Flagship San José plant: up to 90 t/day, co-developed with Pedregal; second plant in York, Pennsylvania (~1 t/hour, with the Alliance and Georgia Pacific); Cape Town plant (2023); pilots in Mexico, UK, Australia, NZ, Samoa and Hong Kong; used by Habitat for Humanity in Latin American social housing; project cost premium of only ~0.11%; vision of expansion to 20+ cities
